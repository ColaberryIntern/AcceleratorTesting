The finding in one paragraph

Producing the actual design — deriving components from the paragraph, writing architecture.md, building the BLUEPRINT object — took between 1:42 and 2:14 for every idea tested, from a five-component CRUD app to a twelve-component real-time AI system. Idea complexity barely moved it. What took time was regenerating roughly 1,900 lines of site scaffolding that is the same for every student: 3 minutes 39 seconds for site.js alone. Three quarters of the class's waiting is spent recreating identical plumbing.

Method, and what it does not tell you

Read this before quoting any number downstream.

What was actually measured. Five idea paragraphs, each written with a day-one sentence as the prompt requires, were run through phases 1–2 of the prompt (design the system, write architecture.md, build the data object) with wall-clock timestamps taken between each. The scaffolding phase was then measured once in full, by generating a complete working site.js for one of the five.

What was modelled rather than measured. site.css is byte-identical across ideas, so it was copied rather than regenerated; its cost is estimated at 86 seconds from the measured generation rate of site.js (4.3 s/KB × 19.8 KB). The nine page shells were script-generated in 15 seconds; hand-generated they would be nearer 40. Both estimates are stated inline wherever they appear.

Why your students will be slower, not faster. These timings come from one operator on Claude Opus 5 who had already built this system twice and therefore knew the target shape. That is a real confound and it runs in one direction: treat every machine-time number here as a floor. A cold run has to make the same decisions from scratch, and a student will re-run at least once.

Not measured at all: student typing speed, how long it takes somebody to write a good paragraph, how many will get it wrong on the first attempt, and rate-limit behaviour if a cohort shares one API organisation. The last one is the biggest unknown and is called out at the end.

Recommended class block

Budget 45 minutes for the first supervised run. That absorbs one revision, a slow paragraph writer, and the two or three students who will need help. Anything under 30 minutes will leave a third of the room unfinished.

0–8 min
You talk, nobody types. Explain the day-one sentence and why it is the only part of the paragraph that matters. Show one finished blueprint. Do not distribute the prompt yet.
8–20 min
Paragraphs only. Students write and you read over shoulders. This is the highest-leverage twelve minutes in the session — a weak paragraph produces a generic diagram no amount of runtime fixes.
20–28 min
Paste and run. Roughly eight minutes of machine time. Use it: walk the room, or have students predict their own component list and compare afterwards.
28–40 min
Read the output. Command Center, then Architecture, then Assumptions. The open question is usually the most interesting thing on the page.
40–45 min
Buffer. Re-runs and stragglers.

The rule that impressed me most

Four of five ideas came back with no AI layer at all — including the warehouse watchdog, where "detect anomalies" is exactly the phrase that makes people reach for a model. The design came back as counts, null ratios and comparisons against a stored baseline, with an explicit note that reaching for a model would add cost and unexplainability to a problem arithmetic solves exactly. That is the single most valuable thing this prompt teaches, and it is surviving contact with real ideas.

The one change that would halve your class time

Decision for the DRI

site.js and site.css are idea-independent. Every student generates roughly 1,900 identical lines and waits five and a half minutes for them. Shipping those two files as a starter kit — so students generate only architecture.md, blueprint.js and nine tiny shells — would cut model time from about 7½ minutes to under 2½, and cut the class block from 45 minutes to roughly 30.

The trade-off is real and it is the one you deliberately locked. A starter kit means the prompt is no longer self-contained: it needs files distributed to every machine, versioned, and kept in step with the prompt. That is exactly the coupling v1.0 was written to avoid. This is a scheduling-versus-portability call, not a technical one, and it is yours.

If you keep v1.0 as locked

Nothing needs to change — just budget the 45 minutes and use the generation window as teaching time rather than dead time. The scaffolding wait is genuinely idle for the student, so fill it deliberately.

Risks worth naming before you run this with twenty people

RiskWhy it mattersMitigation
high Shared API organisation Twenty simultaneous runs each generating ~50 KB is the one thing not tested here. Rate limiting would hit the whole room at once, mid-generation. Run a three-student pilot at the same moment before the full session, or stagger starts by five minutes.
high Weak paragraphs A paragraph with no day-one sentence produces a generic three-tier diagram. Runtime is unaffected — the student simply gets a worse result and does not know why. Gate on the paragraph. Nobody pastes the prompt until someone has read their sentence.
medium No internet Mermaid and Chart.js come from a CDN. Illustrations, search and the keyless Ask mode work offline; the three CDN diagrams degrade to an explanatory message. Ask for the offline build in advance if the room's connection is unreliable.
medium Model and effort settings vary All numbers here are Claude Opus 5. A student on a different model or effort setting will not match them. Standardise the setting at the start of the session, or accept a wider spread.
low Scaffolding bugs 1,900 lines regenerated per student is 1,900 chances for a defect. One was found during development: a const at top level is not a window property, and every page threw until it was fixed. The prompt now warns about that specific trap. Others may exist.

What this audit did not do

Only one of the five ideas was built all the way to a running site; the other four stopped after the data object, because the scaffolding phase is demonstrably idea-independent and building it five times would have measured the same thing five times. No student ran anything — every timing is one operator's. Nothing was measured under concurrency. And no judgement is offered here on whether the designs are good, only that they are structurally valid and internally consistent.
